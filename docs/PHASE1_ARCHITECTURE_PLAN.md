## 0. Read This First — Six Things That Shape the Plan

Before the section-by-section plan, five findings that materially affect what you should build. They're up front because four of them are things you'd otherwise discover late and expensively.

### 0.1 Patient identity — the 12-digit Health ID (DECIDED)

**Decision:** the patient identifier is a **system-issued 12-digit Health ID**, generated by RuralAI. It is Aadhaar-*shaped* (12 digits, familiar to rural users and staff, easy to read aloud and verify) but it is **not** Aadhaar, is not derived from Aadhaar, and the word "Aadhaar" appears nowhere in the schema, the API, the UI, or the documentation.

Call it the **RuralAI Health ID (RHID)** throughout. All patient details hang off it.

**Why this is the right call, not just the requested one:** it gives you the entire usability benefit of a familiar 12-digit key with none of the legal exposure. Under the **Aadhaar Act 2016** (as amended 2019), storing raw Aadhaar numbers is restricted to AUA/KUA-licensed entities, and the **DPDP Act 2023** layers consent and breach-notification duties on top for health data. A number you issue yourself is your own data, governed only by DPDP — a far smaller surface.

**Design specifics:**
- **`rhid`** — 12 digits, system-generated, with a check digit (Verhoeff or Luhn) so mistyped IDs are rejected at entry rather than creating a duplicate patient. This matters more than it sounds: transcription errors are the #1 source of duplicate records in field EMRs.
- **Not sequential.** Random within the 12-digit space with collision check on insert. Sequential IDs leak patient volume and let anyone enumerate records.
- **Internal PK stays a UUID.** The RHID is the *human-facing* key and is unique-indexed; foreign keys use the UUID. This is standard practice and costs nothing — it means an RHID could be reissued or corrected without cascading through every table.
- **If a patient already has an ABHA ID**, store it in an optional `abha_id` column for future ABDM interoperability. Not required, not the key.
- **No government ID number is stored** — not in plaintext, not hashed, not last-4. Nothing to leak, nothing to defend. This closes §0.1 as a risk item entirely.

**On stage this is a strength:** "we issue our own health ID so no citizen ID data ever enters the system" is a clean, confident answer to the privacy question that someone in that room will ask.  → §B.2, §C.6

### 0.2 Offline PWA — DEFERRED, but stay offline-ready

**Decision: not built in this version.** The offline-capable PWA ships after the current version is deployed.

That's a reasonable call — it's a substantial subsystem (sync queue, conflict resolution, service worker lifecycle) and it isn't what wins the Summit demo. But two cheap things should be done **now**, because retrofitting them later is expensive and doing them now costs almost nothing:

1. **All writes go through a single client-side data-access module.** When offline queueing arrives, it slots in behind that one module instead of being threaded through 40 components. This is a structural decision, not a feature.
2. **No clinical write depends on a synchronous server response to proceed.** The UI advances optimistically and reconciles. This is good UX on a slow rural connection *today*, independent of offline support.

**What you should still build now:** graceful degradation. A dropped connection mid-capture must show a clear "reconnecting, your data is safe" state rather than losing the form. That is a one-day feature and it prevents the most likely on-stage embarrassment.  → §F.4

### 0.3 Medicine: lowest-severity cases only, and never a model (DECIDED)

**Decision — medicine is now scoped much more tightly, and this is a good decision:**

| Tier | Medicine behaviour |
|---|---|
| **LOW** (very minor only) | AI suggests from a small OTC formulary → **queued for doctor review** (§0.6). |
| **MEDIUM** | **No AI medicine at all.** Video consultation → **doctor issues a PDF prescription** from the Doctor Portal → it appears in the Clinical Assistant portal. |
| **HIGH** | **No medicine.** Referral only. |

This narrowing removes most of the liability surface in one stroke — the system only ever suggests a drug for the mildest presentations, and a licensed doctor authors every real prescription. Say this explicitly in the pitch; it's the answer to "what if the AI gets the medicine wrong."

**Within LOW, the mechanism must still be deterministic.** A generative model emitting drug names and dosages is an unbounded liability surface: hallucinated doses, missed contraindications, paediatric weight errors. So: a **rules engine over a small, hand-curated, clinician-signed-off OTC formulary**, with hard gates on age, weight, pregnancy/lactation, allergy and interactions. The LLM may *retrieve and explain* an entry; it may never *author* one.

Because the scope is now only the mildest cases, the formulary gets smaller too — realistically **10–20 entries** (paracetamol, ORS, zinc, antiseptics, basic antihistamines). A shorter list is faster to get clinician sign-off on, which moves your critical path forward.  → §D.2

### 0.4 Triage must be monotonically escalating

The LOW/MEDIUM/HIGH tier decides whether a patient goes home with paracetamol or gets referred to a hospital. A model that under-triages kills someone.

**Recommendation:** compute `final_tier = max(deterministic_rule_tier, model_tier)`. A deterministic red-flag layer (vitals thresholds, IMCI danger signs, age extremes, pregnancy) can only ever *raise* the tier. The model can raise it; the model can **never lower** it below what the rules say.

This one architectural constraint is what makes the system defensible in a room containing doctors.  → §D.6

### 0.5 Backend: Supabase confirmed (DECIDED)

**Decision: Supabase / Postgres**, not MongoDB. The `mongo-data/` folder in the Phase 2 skeleton becomes **`db-data/`** (local Postgres container volume), and `models/` holds Drizzle schema + Zod types rather than Mongoose schemas. Everything else in your repo structure is unchanged. Full reasoning in §A; the deciding factor is native Postgres RLS for the role model in §3.8.

**Still outstanding:** your Phase 2 message cuts off mid-sentence at *"Doctor (day-wise review of assigned LOW cases"*. Your review answered the doctor-review question directly (§0.6), so this is no longer blocking — but re-send the full Phase 2 block when convenient, in case it carries constraints not present elsewhere.

### 0.6 The doctor review loop is a closed loop (DECIDED — new requirement)

This is a **change to the workflow**, and a good one. Previously the doctor's LOW-case review was a terminal action. It is now a **round trip back to the Clinical Assistant**:

```
LOW assessment → doctor's daily review queue
    ├── nothing uneven  → doctor APPROVES the AI response as correct for that patient
    │                     → case closed, assistant notified, AI output stands as reviewed
    └── something uneven → doctor FLAGS it with a clinical note
                          → case is FORWARDED BACK to the Clinical Assistant
                          → assistant sees the doctor's note + corrected instruction
                          → assistant acts on the doctor's guidance, not the AI's
```

**Why this matters architecturally, beyond being what you asked for:**

- It closes the safety loop. The AI's output is never final until a human confirms it, and when a human disagrees the correction reaches the person actually treating the patient. That is the difference between "a doctor reviewed it" and "a doctor's correction changed care."
- **Every flag is a labelled training example.** A doctor saying "this was wrong, here's why" is the highest-quality supervision signal you will ever get, and it arrives free as a by-product of normal operation. This is the flywheel described in §D.1 — and the reason §B.2 keeps `doctor_reviews` as a separate table rather than a status column.
- Your **doctor override rate** becomes a real, measurable quality metric. When an incubator asks "how do you know your AI is any good," a falling override rate over time is the answer.

**Schema impact** (§B.2): `doctor_reviews.action` becomes `approve | flag_to_assistant | refer`, with `clinical_note` **required** when the action is `flag_to_assistant` — a flag with no explanation is useless to the assistant. Flagging emits a realtime notification to the originating assistant and moves the visit to an `awaiting_assistant_action` status, so flagged cases can't be silently lost.

**UX impact** (§F.2): the assistant portal needs a **"Doctor feedback"** section, visually distinct and higher-priority than the recent-patients stack. A flagged case must be impossible to miss.

# A. Tech Stack & Backend/Database Decision

## A.1 What we're actually optimising for

Ranked, because the ranking decides the answer:

1. **Live multi-device realtime under demo conditions.** One doctor device plus several assistant devices, in sync, on unknown venue wifi, in front of a governor. Failure here is total failure.
2. **Role-based data isolation that survives scrutiny.** Doctors see only assigned cases; Admin sees everything except patient clinical edits. Enforced at the *data layer*, not just application code — "our API checks permissions" is a sentence; "the database physically will not return rows you aren't entitled to" is a guarantee.
3. **Build velocity to the Summit.** You're one team. Every hour hand-rolling auth is an hour not spent on the triage engine.
4. **Cost at demo scale ≈ ₹0**, then predictable at commercial scale.
5. **Exit velocity if incubated.** No lock-in requiring a rewrite.

## A.3 Recommendation

> **Supabase (managed Postgres + Auth + Storage + Realtime) as the system of record, with a Node/Express "RuralAI Core API" service layer in front of it for everything Supabase can't or shouldn't do.**

**Supabase owns:**
- Postgres — all clinical and operational data
- Auth — identity, JWTs, sessions, MFA, admin-provisioned accounts
- Storage — wound images, prescription scans, report scans, voice recordings
- Realtime — live subscriptions for the doctor queue, assistant dashboard, notification feed
- **RLS** — the enforcement layer for §3.8's role model

**The Express Core API owns:**
- **AI orchestration** — triage engine, LLM calls, prompt/version management (server-held keys; never client-side)
- **The deterministic safety layer** — red-flag rules, formulary gates, dosage checks. Server-only, auditable, versioned
- **OCR pipeline** — upload → preprocess → extract → structure → persist
- **STT/TTS/translation proxy** — key custody again
- **IoT ingest** — BLE/serial payload normalisation, device registry
- **Video scheduling and the 5-minute tolerance window** — needs server-authoritative time and a job runner
- **Background jobs** — doctor review queue, risk re-evaluation, call reminders
- **Socket.IO** — signalling and ephemeral events that aren't plain table changes
- **Hospital referral matching** — geospatial + bed-availability logic

### Why this split rather than one or the other

**Why not pure Supabase (client straight to DB)?** The AI layer needs secret keys, deterministic server-side safety gates, background jobs and third-party orchestration. Edge Functions could carry some of it, but you'd write the same logic in a more constrained runtime with worse local debugging — and your repo convention is already Express-shaped. Fighting that buys nothing.

**Why not pure custom Express + Mongo (what the Phase 2 skeleton implies)?** Three reasons, in severity order:

1. **You lose RLS.** Every permission bug becomes a data-leak bug, in a *healthcare* app, in front of a government. With Postgres RLS, a forgotten `WHERE doctor_id = ...` returns zero rows instead of every patient in the state. That is the difference between a bug and an incident.
2. **You hand-build realtime.** Doable — you want Socket.IO anyway — but you then own consistency, reconnection, backfill-on-reconnect, and multi-instance fan-out behind a load balancer. That's a week you don't have, on the exact subsystem whose failure is most visible on stage.
3. **The domain is relational.** Patient → visits → vitals → symptoms → documents → assessments → consultations → referrals, plus audit trails and time-series vitals. In Mongo you either denormalise (and fight consistency) or reinvent joins.

**Why not Firebase?** Two disqualifiers. Read-priced billing on a product whose core screens are live-updating dashboards is a cost curve that worsens exactly as you succeed. And Firestore lock-in is real — there's no `pg_dump` equivalent, and "can you migrate off?" is a question incubators and enterprise buyers *do* ask.

### The main trade-off, stated plainly

**You run two systems instead of one, and you must stay disciplined about which enforces what.** The specific hazard: the Core API holds a Supabase **service-role key, which bypasses RLS entirely**. Route reads through it carelessly and you've silently disabled your best security control while believing it's on.

**Mitigation — a hard rule for Phase 2:**
- The **client** talks to Supabase directly (anon key + user JWT) for reads and simple realtime subscriptions → RLS enforced by Postgres.
- The **Core API** uses the service-role key **only** for writes requiring server-side logic or trusted computation (assessment results, tier assignment, audit entries, admin provisioning).
- Every service-role code path gets an explicit, tested authorisation check **and** an audit-log row. No exceptions. This is a code-review gate, not a guideline.

## A.7 Migrations without the Supabase MCP (DECIDED)

The Supabase MCP server is installed but unauthenticated, and authorising it needs an interactive OAuth flow. **We're skipping it.** It changes nothing about the architecture — it's a convenience layer, not a dependency.

**How schema work ships instead:**

- Every schema change is a **numbered SQL migration file** in `db/migrations/` (`0001_init.sql`, `0002_rls_policies.sql`, …), committed to the repo.
- Applied either by pasting into the **Supabase dashboard → SQL Editor**, or via `supabase db push` once the CLI is set up.
- **This is better practice than MCP-driven schema changes anyway.** Migrations in version control are reviewable, replayable, and reversible; ad-hoc MCP changes are none of those. For a healthcare system where RLS policies are a security control, having every policy change visible in git history is the correct default — and it's what you'd need for any audit later regardless.

The trade-off is a manual copy-paste step per migration. That's a few seconds, and it buys a clean audit trail.

**Also unaffected:** the `supabase` and `supabase-postgres-best-practices` skills stay available, since the plugin remains enabled. Those are the parts that actually help during the build.

# B. Database & Data Model Strategy

Entities, relationships and access-control logic only — no SQL.

## B.1 Design principles

1. **Visit-centric, not patient-centric.** Everything clinical hangs off a `visit` (an encounter), never directly off a patient. A patient who returns three times has three visits, each with its own vitals, symptoms, assessment and outcome. This is what makes the longitudinal risk detector (§D.5) possible at all, and it's how every real EMR is modelled.
2. **Append-only for anything clinical.** Vitals, symptoms, assessments and doctor decisions are never updated in place — corrections create a new row that supersedes the old one. A healthcare system that can't show what was known at decision time is not defensible.
3. **AI output and human decision are separate tables.** Not a status column on one row. Two tables, two authors, two timestamps. This is the schema-level expression of the brief's "never blur AI suggestion with doctor's decision" requirement — and being able to say "they're physically different records" is a much stronger answer than "we show a different colour."
4. **Region is a first-class dimension.** State → district → facility, present on staff and facilities, because Admin's scoping (§3.8) and doctor-selection load balancing (§3.6) both key off it.

- **`consultations`** — `visit_id`, `doctor_id`, `scheduled_at`, `tolerance_expires_at` (scheduled + 5 min, per §3.3), `status` (`scheduled` / `ringing` / `active` / `completed` / `missed` / `reassigned`), `provider`, `provider_room_id`, `joined_at`, `ended_at`, `recording_consent`.
- **`doctor_availability`** — time-slot rows driving the load-balanced assignment in §3.6 MEDIUM.
- **`referrals`** — HIGH-tier output: `visit_id`, `target_facility_id`, `reason`, `distance_km`, `bill_id`, `printed_at`.
- **`bills`** — for the HIGH-tier print step, incl. line items and the "danger zone cleared" timestamp.

### Cross-cutting

- **`notifications`** — `recipient_profile_id`, `type`, `payload`, `read_at`, `delivered_via[]`. Realtime subscribes here; this is also the durable backstop so a notification missed while offline is still there on reconnect. Do not rely on the socket alone.
- **`audit_log`** — `actor_profile_id`, `action`, `entity_type`, `entity_id`, `before`, `after`, `ip`, `user_agent`, `at`. **Append-only, no deletes, no updates, enforced by trigger.** Every service-role write lands here (§A.3).
- **`consents`** — DPDP-mandated: `patient_id`, `purpose`, `granted_by`, `granted_at`, `revoked_at`, `capture_method`. For low-literacy patients this is often verbal, so `capture_method` should support a recorded verbal consent stored as an audio artifact.
- **`devices`** — IoT registry (§E.4): `device_type`, `protocol`, `identifier`, `facility_id`, `last_seen_at`, `calibration_due_at`.

## B.3 How realtime maps onto this

Three subscription patterns, deliberately narrow — broad subscriptions are how you accidentally leak data and how you melt a free tier:

| Consumer | Subscribes to | Filtered by |
|---|---|---|
| Doctor portal | `visits` (status changes), `consultations`, `notifications`, `doctor_reviews` queue | `doctor_id = me` OR `district_id = my district` for unassigned pool |
| Assistant portal | `visits` (own facility), `ai_assessments` (completion), `notifications`, `consultations` | `facility_id = my facility` |
| Admin console | Aggregate counters, `audit_log` tail | scope from `admins.scope_level` |

**Critical detail:** Supabase Realtime respects RLS on the replication stream. Filters are therefore not just an optimisation — an assistant physically cannot subscribe to another facility's visits, even with a hand-crafted client. That property is worth demonstrating live if anyone asks about security.

Ephemeral events that should **not** be table writes go over Socket.IO instead: call ringing, "doctor is reviewing this case", typing indicators, device-connection status. Writing those to Postgres would generate write amplification for data with a two-second useful life.

## B.4 How RLS maps onto this

The pattern, applied uniformly:

- A helper reads `role`, `facility_id`, `district_id`, `state_id` from the authenticated user's JWT claims (populated at login from `profiles`).
- **Patients / visits / clinical tables:** Clinical Assistant → rows where `facility_id` matches theirs. Doctor → rows where they are assigned, or which are in their district's unassigned pool. Admin → read-only within their geographic scope, **with clinical write explicitly denied** (§3.8's "Admin cannot edit patient data" becomes a policy, not a UI convention — the button being hidden is not a control).
- **Documents / storage:** Storage policies mirror table policies, keyed on the owning visit's facility. Signed URLs, short TTL, never public buckets. A leaked wound-image URL is a personal-data breach.
- **`ai_assessments` / `ai_recommendations`:** read per the visit rules; **insert only via service role**. No client may author an assessment — that would let a compromised client fabricate a LOW tier.
- **`doctor_reviews`:** insert allowed only where `doctor_id = auth.uid()` and the doctor is actually assigned to that visit. Update denied entirely; corrections are new rows.
- **`audit_log`:** insert-only for everyone; select restricted to Admin and a future Auditor role (§C.1). No update, no delete, for anyone, ever.

## B.5 Where this could bite

- **RLS on hot paths costs query time.** The doctor dashboard joining across five policy-checked tables can get slow. Mitigation: materialised views or a purpose-built `doctor_queue_view`, refreshed on write, with its own simpler policy.
- **Append-only grows fast.** Vitals and assessments accumulate. Partition `vitals` and `audit_log` by month before commercial scale — cheap to plan now, painful to retrofit.
- **`input_snapshot_hash` requires canonical serialisation.** Field-order instability silently breaks reproducibility. Fix the serialisation format once, in one shared utility, and test it.

Belt and braces, because this is the one that a government security review will actually test.

## C.3 Admin-only provisioning — how it's enforced

Four layers, because one is not enough:

1. **Supabase Auth signup disabled at the project level.** No public `signUp` path exists. This is a dashboard setting, and it should be captured in your infrastructure notes so it can't be silently re-enabled.
2. **Account creation only via the Core API's admin endpoint,** which uses the service-role key and requires a valid admin JWT with sufficient scope. The endpoint validates that the caller's scope contains the target's district.
3. **A database trigger on `profiles`** rejecting any insert where `created_by_admin_id` is null or references a non-admin. Even a direct DB write can't create an orphan account.
4. **Invitation flow, not password-setting.** Admin creates the account and the system emails/SMSes a single-use, time-limited invite; the staff member sets their own password. **The admin never knows the credential.** This matters: "who could have logged in as that doctor?" needs to have exactly one answer.

## C.4 Session and credential handling

- **JWT access tokens, short-lived (~1 hour), with refresh-token rotation** and reuse detection — a replayed refresh token invalidates the whole family.
- **Custom JWT claims** carrying `role`, `facility_id`, `district_id`, `state_id`, injected by a Supabase auth hook at token issue. RLS policies read claims, not extra table lookups — this is what keeps policy evaluation fast.
- **Web tokens in httpOnly, Secure, SameSite=Lax cookies.** Not localStorage: an XSS in a page that renders OCR'd text from an uploaded document should not be able to exfiltrate a session.
- **MFA (TOTP) mandatory for all admin roles**, optional-but-encouraged for doctors. A super_admin account without MFA is the whole system's single point of failure.
- **Device binding for Clinical Assistants.** Shared clinic devices are the norm, so bind sessions to a registered facility device and auto-logout after inactivity — a village health centre tablet left unlocked on a bench is a realistic threat, not a theoretical one.
- **Rate limiting** on login (per IP and per account), assessment runs, and file uploads.
- **Break-glass access** for `super_admin` — permitted, but always writes a high-severity audit entry and fires an alert. Emergency access that leaves no trace isn't emergency access, it's a backdoor.

## C.5 The "secret admin login"

Your §3.8 asks for a secret non-public admin login. Security-by-obscurity is not itself a control, so implement it as obscurity **plus** real controls:

- Admin login lives on an unlinked, non-indexed route (`robots.txt` + `noindex`, absent from all public navigation).
- **MFA required.** Non-negotiable.
- Optional IP allow-list for `super_admin` during the demo window — cheap, and meaningfully reduces risk on the day.
- Failed-attempt lockout with exponential backoff.
- Every admin login writes an audit entry and fires a realtime alert to your own channel.
- The route being hidden is presentation; MFA and the audit trail are the actual security. Be ready to say exactly that if asked on stage — it's the answer that lands well.

## C.6 Patient identity — the 12-digit Health ID (DECIDED)

Restating §0.1 concretely, since it's an auth concern too:

- **Internal primary key:** system-generated UUID. All foreign keys use this.
- **Human-facing identifier:** **`rhid`** — a system-issued **12-digit RuralAI Health ID**, unique-indexed, with a check digit so typos are rejected at entry. This is what the assistant types, what the patient is told, and what everything hangs off.
- **Generated, not collected.** RuralAI issues the number. It is not derived from, linked to, or validated against any government ID.
- **No government ID number is stored** — not raw, not hashed, not partially. The word "Aadhaar" does not appear in the schema, API, UI or logs.
- **`abha_id`:** optional column, populated only if the patient volunteers one, reserved for future ABDM interoperability. Never required.
- **Emergency bypass (§3.8):** issues an RHID immediately (generation is instant and needs no external lookup — a real advantage of owning the identifier) with `emergency_registration = true`, and queues a task to complete demographics later. The urgent path is never blocked on identity.
- **Search:** the assistant's "find existing patient" flow searches by RHID, name + village, or phone. RHID search is exact-match on a check-digit-validated input, which makes it fast and error-resistant.

**Security note:** because RHID is the lookup key and it's guessable in principle (12 digits is a large space, but enumeration is still worth preventing), RHID lookup is rate-limited per user and every lookup writes an audit entry. A Clinical Assistant repeatedly probing IDs outside their facility is a detectable pattern, and RLS means they'd get zero rows anyway.

> **Resolved:** the §0.1 legal-exposure item is now closed. Nothing in the system constitutes government identity data, so the Aadhaar Act question does not arise. DPDP obligations (consent, purpose limitation, breach notification) still apply to health data and are handled via the `consents` table.

# D. AI Model Training Workflow

Process and pipeline only — no code. The organising principle for this whole section:

> **Nothing probabilistic is allowed to make a safety-critical decision alone.** Models generate hypotheses; deterministic rules set floors and gates; humans hold final authority. Every one of the five components below is designed around that sentence.

A second framing point, which matters for how you present this: **you are not training five models before the Summit, and you should not claim to be.** You are building a *system* in which four components are LLM/RAG-driven with deterministic guardrails, one is a pure rules engine, and all five have a defined path to trained models with real data. That is both what's achievable and what's honest — and "here is our path from retrieval-augmented reasoning to a validated clinical model" is a stronger story to an incubator than an unverifiable claim of a trained diagnostic model.

## D.1 Model 1 — Symptoms → Preliminary Differential

**What it must produce:** a ranked differential (not a diagnosis) with confidence, plus the evidence that drove it.

**Demo-stage approach: RAG + structured LLM reasoning, not fine-tuning.**

- **Retrieval corpus:** WHO IMCI (Integrated Management of Childhood Illness) guidelines, India's Standard Treatment Guidelines for primary care, NHM/ASHA field manuals, and the CHO/Ayushman Bharat HWC protocol handbooks. These are public, authoritative, and — critically — *the exact documents a state health official already recognises*. Citing IMCI on stage is worth more than citing a Kaggle dataset.
- **Method:** chunk and embed the corpus; retrieve on normalised symptom codes + demographics + vitals; pass retrieved protocol text to the LLM with a strictly structured output schema (differential list, confidence, supporting findings, contradicting findings, red flags observed, recommended tier).
- **Why not fine-tune now:** fine-tuning needs thousands of labelled cases you don't have, produces a model you can't easily explain, and — the decisive point — *loses the citation*. RAG lets every output point at the protocol paragraph that justified it. In a clinical setting, traceability beats marginal accuracy.

**Data needed for the trained version:** labelled symptom→diagnosis cases with demographics and outcomes.
**Realistic sourcing at your budget:**
- **DDXPlus** (~1.3M synthetic patient cases with differentials, open licence) — good for structure and evaluation harness, synthetic so not sufficient alone.
- **SymCat** symptom-disease co-occurrence data.
- **MIMIC-IV** — real, high quality, but requires PhysioNet credentialing + a signed DUA + CITI training. Free, but budget two to four weeks for approval. Worth starting now.
- **Your own accumulating data**, which is the real answer: every assessment plus its doctor review is a labelled example. This is the flywheel, and designing for it now (§B.2's `doctor_reviews` table) is why the schema looks the way it does.

**Evolution path:** RAG-only → RAG + a lightweight symptom-normalisation classifier (mapping colloquial multilingual descriptions to SNOMED/ICD-11 codes, which is where a small fine-tuned model genuinely helps) → supervised differential model trained on accumulated doctor-adjudicated cases, evaluated against held-out real cases.

> **Needs clinical sign-off:** the retrieval corpus selection and the prompt's clinical framing must be reviewed by a practising physician before any demo. Not optional.

## D.2 Model 2 — Disease → Medicine: LOW tier only, and a rules engine, not a model

Restating §0.3 because it's the most important design decision in this section.

**Scope (DECIDED):** medicine suggestion exists **only for the lowest-severity LOW cases**. MEDIUM produces a doctor-issued PDF prescription after video consultation (§E.2b). HIGH produces a referral and no medicine at all. This scoping does more for safety than any amount of model tuning.

**Within LOW, this component must be deterministic.** Structure:

- **A curated OTC formulary table** — deliberately tiny now that scope is LOW-only (realistically **10–20 entries**): paracetamol, ORS, zinc, basic antihistamines, topical antiseptics. Each entry carries indication, age bands, weight-based dosing, max daily dose, duration limit, contraindications, interactions, pregnancy/lactation category, and **the citation for the guideline it came from**. A shorter list is also faster to get signed off, which shortens your critical path.
- **Hard gates evaluated before any recommendation is emitted:** age band, weight (required for paediatric dosing — no weight, no paediatric dose, full stop), known allergy match, pregnancy status, current medications from OCR'd prescriptions, and renal/hepatic flags from history.
- **Any failed gate suppresses the recommendation and escalates the tier.** It does not degrade to a warning.
- **The LLM's only role** is selecting candidate formulary entries and phrasing the explanation in the patient's language. It cannot invent an entry, a dose, or a duration. Output is validated against the formulary table before it's persisted — an unmatched drug name is a hard error, logged as a safety event, not silently dropped.

**The schema-level enforcement** (§B.2): a medication row in `ai_recommendations` with a null `rule_source_id` should be rejected by a database constraint. Make it structurally impossible to persist an unsourced drug recommendation.

**Everything outside the OTC formulary is not a recommendation** — it's an escalation to a doctor. Prescription-only medicines are never suggested by the system to an assistant, in any tier.

> **Needs clinical sign-off — the hard blocker of this project.** The formulary must be reviewed and signed by a registered physician, and that sign-off recorded (`senior_doctor` role, §C.1). Until signed, the demo runs with the formulary clearly labelled as unvalidated. I will not populate real dosing data as though it were validated — per your Section 0 rule, that's exactly the "fabricating realistic-looking medical data" case.

> **→ NEEDS FROM YOU:** a clinician contact who can review the formulary, and any protocol references your state health department already endorses. This is on the critical path to the Summit; start it before the code.

## D.3 Model 3 — Wound Image → Extent / Associated Condition

**Demo-stage approach:** vision-capable LLM with a **structured clinical rubric** rather than free-form description. The model scores defined axes — approximate size, depth indication, tissue appearance, surrounding erythema, signs of infection (purulence, spreading redness, necrosis), foreign body visible, bleeding character — and those axis scores feed the deterministic tier rules. Free-form image description is not clinically usable and should never be shown to an assistant as an assessment.

**Data for the trained version:**
- **DFUC** (Diabetic Foot Ulcer Challenge), **AZH wound care dataset**, **Medetec** wound image database — the standard open options.
- **ISIC** for skin lesions, if you extend to dermatological presentations.
- All are small and Western-skewed.

**The bias problem, stated explicitly because it will be asked about:** most public wound and dermatology datasets substantially under-represent brown and black skin. A model trained naively on them will detect erythema less reliably on your actual patient population — which is a real, documented clinical-safety failure mode in dermatological AI, not a hypothetical. Mitigations: (a) rubric-based assessment with conservative thresholds rather than end-to-end classification at demo stage; (b) deliberate collection of a locally-representative labelled set as you deploy; (c) making the model's confidence and its known limitation visible in the UI rather than hidden.

**Evolution path:** rubric + vision LLM → fine-tuned segmentation/classification model on a locally-collected, clinician-labelled set → validated against prospective outcomes. Note that anything claiming diagnostic performance eventually needs a clinical evaluation study; plan for it, don't claim it early.

**Hard rule:** wound imaging can only ever *raise* the tier. There is no image finding that downgrades a case.

## D.4 Model 4 — Test/Report Analysis

Deliberately the least "AI" of the five, because it doesn't need to be:

1. **OCR** the report (§E.3) → extract analyte name, value, unit, reference range.
2. **Deterministic comparison** against reference ranges, age- and sex-adjusted. Out-of-range flags are computed arithmetically, not inferred. A haemoglobin of 7.2 g/dL is anaemia by definition, and a model should not be involved in noticing that.
3. **LLM layer** handles only: mapping messy lab naming variants to canonical analytes, and generating the plain-language, patient-language narrative.
4. **Abnormal flags feed the rule layer**, contributing to tier escalation.

**Data needed:** a canonical analyte dictionary with reference ranges (LOINC provides the coding; ranges are lab- and population-specific and need clinical review), plus a corpus of real Indian lab report layouts for OCR tuning.

> **→ NEEDS REAL DATA SOURCE:** sample lab report formats from labs your target facilities actually use. Until then, the OCR templates are placeholder and marked as such. Indian lab reports vary wildly in layout; this is a genuine engineering problem, not a solved one.

## D.5 Model 5 — Longitudinal Risk Detector

The most interesting component and the strongest differentiator — it's the one thing a single-visit teleconsult app cannot do.

**Demo-stage approach: an explainable composite score, not a black box.**

- **Baseline layer:** a NEWS2/MEWS-style early-warning score computed from current vitals. These are clinically validated, published, explainable, and require no training data. Using an established score rather than an invented one is the credibility move here.
- **Trend layer:** deterministic features over the patient's visit history — repeat presentations within a window, unresolved symptoms persisting beyond expected duration, weight trajectory, BP trend, recurring fever, escalating pain scores.
- **Pattern layer:** rule-based detection of concerning combinations drawn from published screening criteria (e.g. persistent cough + weight loss + night sweats → TB screening pathway; polydipsia + polyuria + weight loss → diabetes screening). These are protocol lookups, not learned patterns, and each cites its guideline.
- **Output:** a score plus `contributing_factors[]` — always. A risk flag without its reasons is not actionable by a health worker and not defensible to a doctor.

**Trained version:** gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost/LightGBM) over engineered longitudinal features, with SHAP for per-prediction attribution. Deliberately *not* deep learning — for tabular clinical data GBMs match or beat neural nets, train on modest data, and remain explainable, and explainability is a hard requirement here rather than a preference.

**Data:** your own accumulating visit history, which is exactly why the schema is visit-centric (§B.1). Realistically this needs 12+ months of deployment before it can be trained meaningfully. Say that plainly rather than implying it's already learned.

## D.6 The triage classifier — structure

This is the system's spine, and it is **not** an ML classifier.

```
inputs → [ deterministic red-flag layer ] → rule_tier
       → [ LLM/RAG assessment layer     ] → model_tier
       → final_tier = MAX(rule_tier, model_tier)
       → [ output composer per tier ]
```

**The red-flag layer** is a versioned, unit-tested rule set covering:
- Vitals thresholds (SpO2 < 92%, systolic < 90 or > 180, pulse < 50 or > 120, temp > 39.5°C or < 35°C, RR outside age-adjusted bounds)
- IMCI danger signs for under-5s (inability to drink/feed, lethargy, convulsions, severe chest indrawing)
- Age extremes (infants, elderly) applying stricter thresholds
- Pregnancy
- Symptom red flags (chest pain, breathlessness at rest, altered consciousness, uncontrolled bleeding, suspected fracture, severe dehydration, suicidal ideation)
- Trauma mechanism, burn surface area
- Any failed medication safety gate (§D.2)

**Properties that must hold, and that should be enforced by tests:**
1. **Monotonic escalation.** Rules raise, never lower. `final_tier >= rule_tier` always.
2. **Fail-safe on error.** LLM timeout, malformed output, or unparseable response → default to MEDIUM (doctor contact), never LOW. Degraded AI must never mean "send them home."
3. **Fail-safe on missing data.** Absent vitals do not mean normal vitals. Missing critical inputs raise the tier and prompt the assistant to capture them.
4. **Fully logged.** Every rule evaluation writes to `triage_rule_hits` (§B.2). "Why did it say HIGH?" must always have a precise answer.
5. **Versioned.** Rule set version is stamped on every assessment, so you can reproduce historical decisions.

**Why not a learned triage classifier:** you'd need thousands of labelled cases with outcomes, it would be unexplainable at the moment explanation matters most, and its failure mode — confidently under-triaging an atypical presentation — is the exact failure you cannot accept. The rules layer is boring, and boring is correct here.

## D.7 Validation before anything reaches a doctor or patient

Five gates, in order:

1. **Schema validation.** Every LLM output parsed against a strict Zod schema. Malformed → retry once → fail safe to MEDIUM. Never partially-parsed output shown to a user.
2. **Formulary validation.** Every medication cross-checked against the signed formulary (§D.2). Unmatched entry → hard error + safety event.
3. **Golden case suite.** A fixed set of ~100 constructed cases with clinician-agreed expected tiers, run in CI on every change to prompts, rules or model version. **Any regression that lowers a tier fails the build.** This is your single most valuable safety artifact and it should exist before the demo.
4. **Shadow mode.** Before any tier logic goes live, run it alongside the current logic and diff the outputs. Investigate every disagreement.
5. **Human-in-the-loop, always.** LOW cases queue for daily doctor review (§3.6). MEDIUM routes to a live doctor. HIGH refers to a hospital. **No tier results in autonomous unreviewed treatment** — which is precisely the brief's requirement that the AI never manage cases needing professional attention independently.

Plus continuous monitoring: tier distribution over time (a sudden LOW spike means something broke), doctor override rate per tier (the single best proxy for model quality), time-to-review, and per-assessment cost.

## D.8 Demo-credible → clinically defensible

| Dimension | Governor/Summit demo | Commercialisation |
|---|---|---|
| Diagnosis | RAG over public protocols, cited | Trained + prospectively validated, clinical evaluation study |
| Medicine | Signed OTC formulary, rules-only | Expanded formulary, pharmacist-reviewed, interaction DB licensed |
| Imaging | Rubric + vision LLM, conservative | Fine-tuned on locally-representative labelled data, bias-audited |

Your current positioning — decision *support* for a trained health worker, with mandatory doctor review and no autonomous treatment — is the defensible position, and it should be stated explicitly on the slide and in the UI. Get a regulatory consultant's read before any commercial deployment. Being visibly aware of this in front of a governor is a *strength*, not a liability: it signals you're building a company, not a hackathon project.

# E. Video, Voice, OCR & IoT Integration Plan

## E.1 Multilingual STT / TTS / translation

**Primary recommendation: Bhashini (ULCA APIs), the Government of India's National Language Translation Mission platform.**

Reasoning, in order of weight:
1. **It's the government's own language stack.** Demoing an Indian government AI service inside a state-government showcase is a materially better narrative than showing a US cloud vendor. This is a strategic recommendation as much as a technical one.
2. Genuine coverage of 22 scheduled languages including dialectal variation that Western STT handles poorly.
3. ASR, TTS and translation in one platform.
4. Free or near-free at demo scale.

**Trade-off, stated honestly:** Bhashini's uptime, latency and documentation are less polished than commercial alternatives, and the onboarding involves a registration process. **Therefore: build an adapter interface with a fallback chain.**

| Tier | Provider | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | **Bhashini / ULCA** | Indic ASR, TTS, translation |
| Fallback | **Sarvam AI** | Indian company, strong Indic ASR/TTS, commercial reliability |
| Fallback | **Google Cloud STT/TTS + Translation** | Broadest coverage, highest reliability, paid |
| Offline | **Whisper (small/medium), self-hosted** | Degraded-connectivity capture; transcribe-on-sync |

The `services/` layer defines one `SpeechProvider` interface; providers are swappable by config. Never let provider choice leak into controllers — you will change providers at least once.

**Design points that matter clinically:**
- **Always store the original-language audio and raw transcript** alongside the translation (§B.2). A mistranslated symptom is a clinical error, and the original is your only path to catching it.
- **Read the translated symptom summary back to the patient in their language** for confirmation before running the assessment. This is a five-minute feature that dramatically improves both actual safety and demo impact — it's visible, it's obviously thoughtful, and it directly addresses the low-literacy user.
- **TTS the first-aid instructions** for the assistant, hands-free while treating.
- Symptom normalisation to codes happens *after* translation, on English text, so downstream logic is language-independent.

## E.2 Video consultation — build vs. buy

**Recommendation: buy. Use a prebuilt provider. Do not build WebRTC.**

Custom WebRTC means owning signalling, STUN/TURN infrastructure, NAT traversal, bandwidth adaptation, reconnection, and mobile browser quirks. On rural connectivity. Under live demo conditions. That is weeks of work on the highest-risk-to-demo subsystem, in exchange for zero differentiation — nobody in the audience will award points for your ICE candidate handling, and everybody will notice a call that fails.

**Recommended provider: 100ms.** Indian company with India-region infrastructure (lower latency, and a data-residency answer for a government audience), prebuilt React SDK, good free tier, network-resilience features designed for Indian mobile conditions.

| Option | Verdict |
|---|---|

| Daily.co | Strong alternative, excellent DX, US-centric infra. Good fallback. |

| Custom WebRTC | Not justified. Revisit only at commercial scale with cost pressure. |

**Critical architectural point: notification delivery is completely decoupled from the video provider.** The provider gives you a room; everything else — scheduling, tolerance window, ringing, reassignment — is yours, in `services/` and `sockets/`. Swapping providers should be a config change touching no scheduling logic.

**The 5-minute tolerance window (§3.3), concretely:**
1. MEDIUM tier → `services/consultation-scheduler` picks a doctor by disease category ∩ availability ∩ current load (least-loaded wins), creates a `consultations` row with `scheduled_at` and `tolerance_expires_at = scheduled_at + 5min`.
2. Realtime notification to doctor and assistant simultaneously — via Socket.IO for the immediate ring **and** a `notifications` row for durability (§B.3). A doctor whose socket dropped must still see it on reconnect; relying on the socket alone is how a demo silently fails.
3. Doctor joins → status `active`, tolerance timer cancelled.
4. Doctor doesn't join by `tolerance_expires_at` → a BullMQ delayed job fires → status `missed` → **auto-reassign to the next available doctor**, notify assistant, log it.
5. **One active call per doctor** enforced by a DB constraint plus an application check — not by UI state, which is not a guarantee.
6. Every state transition is a realtime event, so both devices stay in sync. This is the most visually impressive part of a multi-device demo; make the state changes animate.

### E.2b — The MEDIUM-tier prescription handoff (DECIDED, §0.3)

After the consultation, the doctor issues the prescription **as a PDF from the Doctor Portal**, and it appears in the Clinical Assistant's view of that patient. Two production paths, and I'd build both:

- **Generate in-app (recommended default).** The doctor fills a structured form — drug, strength, frequency, duration, instructions — and the system renders a consistent PDF carrying the doctor's name, registration number, signature image and timestamp. **Strictly better than upload:** it's legible (solving the very handwriting problem you're OCR-ing around elsewhere in the system), it's structured data you can reason over later, and it looks professional on stage.
- **Upload an existing PDF.** Fallback for doctors with their own template. Validate MIME and size, scan, store under the same access rules.

**Delivery:** a `prescriptions` row (§B.2) + realtime notification + a signed, short-TTL URL. The assistant can view and print; they cannot edit. RLS restricts read to the assistant on that visit, the issuing doctor, and (later) the patient.

**Immutable once issued.** A correction creates a superseding prescription with `superseded_by` set — never an in-place edit. A prescription that silently changed after being acted on is a serious clinical and legal problem.

**Demo value:** the doctor talks to the assistant on video, fills the form, hits issue — and the PDF appears on the assistant's tablet across the room in real time. That is probably the single most convincing 15 seconds available to you, because it makes the multi-device claim self-evident rather than asserted. Build it well.

> **→ NEEDS YOUR DECISION (cost):** confirm 100ms, and whether you'll create the account (I'll need the API key at build time — I will not stub it per your Section 0 rule). Free tier covers demo comfortably.

## E.3 OCR — prescriptions, reports, ABHA cards

Three distinct problems that people wrongly treat as one:

**(a) ABHA / Ayushman card scanning (§3.8's fast registration).**
**Read the QR code, don't OCR the text.** These cards carry a QR encoding the ABHA number and demographics. QR decoding is deterministic, instant, and ~100% accurate; OCR on a card photo taken in poor light at an angle is none of those. Use OCR only as fallback when no QR is detected. This is the single highest-value practical insight in this section — it turns a flaky feature into a reliable one, and "point the camera, patient is registered" is a great demo beat.

**(b) Printed lab reports.** Google Document AI or Azure Document Intelligence for layout-aware extraction; Tesseract is viable for clean printed text and costs nothing, but struggles with the multi-column tabular layouts Indian labs use. Recommend Document AI for accuracy, Tesseract as a no-cost fallback.

**(c) Handwritten prescriptions — genuinely hard, and be honest about it.** Doctor handwriting is the canonical OCR failure case; no engine solves it reliably. Approach:
- Vision-capable LLM (Claude/Gemini vision) outperforms classical OCR substantially on handwriting.
- **Confidence thresholds with mandatory human confirmation.** Any extracted medication below threshold is presented to the assistant for confirmation, never silently accepted. An OCR error that silently enters a drug name into an interaction check is a patient-safety event.
- Cross-reference extracted drug names against the formulary/drug dictionary; unmatched names are flagged, not guessed.
- **Never auto-populate a clinical field from low-confidence OCR.** This should be a hard rule in the pipeline, enforced by the `needs_human_review` flag on `ocr_results` (§B.2).

**Pipeline shape** (`services/ocr`): upload → `public/temp/` staging → preprocess (deskew, denoise, contrast) → route by `doc_type` to the right engine → extract → structure → confidence-gate → persist to `ocr_results` → surface to assistant for confirmation where flagged → promote to clinical data only after confirmation.

Multi-file handling (§3.7) works on `upload_batch_id`: N files processed in parallel, results aggregated, single confirmation UI for the batch.

## E.4 IoT — pulse oximeter and thermometer, extensibly

**The architectural answer, which is what §3.4 is really asking for:** don't integrate two devices — build a **device abstraction layer** and implement two drivers against it.

**Layers:**

1. **Transport** — how bytes arrive. Three supported from the start:
   - **Web Bluetooth (BLE GATT)** — primary. Works in Chrome on Android and desktop, no native app needed, which matters enormously for a PWA-based deployment.
   - **USB/Serial (Web Serial API)** — for wired clinic devices.
   - **HTTP ingest endpoint** — for gateway/hub devices and, importantly, for anything that can POST. This is the escape hatch that makes future hardware trivial.
2. **Driver** — per device model, translating vendor payloads into a normalised reading. Standard BLE health services help a lot here: **Pulse Oximeter Service (0x1822)** and **Health Thermometer Service (0x1809)** are specified GATT profiles, so a compliant device needs almost no custom code. Non-compliant devices (many cheap oximeters use proprietary characteristics) need a per-model driver — which is exactly what the driver layer is for.
3. **Normalisation** — every driver emits the same shape, modelled on **FHIR Observation** (code, value, unit, effectiveDateTime, device reference, status). Adopting a standard shape now means ABDM/FHIR interoperability later is a mapping exercise, not a rewrite. Worth mentioning on stage: "our vitals are FHIR-shaped" is a sentence health-IT people react to.
4. **Ingest service** (`services/iot`) — validates ranges, rejects implausible values, attaches `device_id` and `capture_method = 'iot_device'`, writes to `vitals`, emits a realtime event so the value appears on the assistant's screen the instant it's measured. That live-appearing vital is a genuinely good demo moment.

**Procedure for integrating a new device class — the repeatable process §3.4 asks for:**
1. Identify transport and, for BLE, enumerate GATT services/characteristics (nRF Connect or similar).
2. Determine whether it implements a standard health service or proprietary characteristics.
3. Write a driver implementing the `DeviceDriver` interface: `connect()`, `subscribe()`, `parse(raw) → Observation[]`, `disconnect()`.
4. Register it in the device registry with its capability declaration (which observation codes it produces).
5. Add parser unit tests using captured raw payloads — critical, since you can't run CI against physical hardware.
6. Add plausibility bounds for its observation types.
7. Register physical units in `devices` with calibration tracking.

**Safety points:** flag readings as device- vs manually-sourced (a certified oximeter's SpO2 and a typed one deserve different confidence in the risk score); reject physiologically implausible values rather than storing them; track calibration due dates, because an uncalibrated device feeding a triage engine is a real hazard.

> **Needs sign-off:** consumer-grade oximeters and thermometers are not always medically certified. For a demo this is fine and should simply be stated. For commercial deployment, devices should be CDSCO-registered, and that requirement belongs in your procurement plan.

> **→ NEEDS FROM YOU:** exact make/model of the oximeter and thermometer you have. I cannot write a working driver against a guessed BLE profile, and I'm not going to fake one. If the hardware isn't in hand yet, I'll build the abstraction layer plus a simulated driver — clearly labelled as simulated — so the pipeline is demonstrable and the real driver drops in later.

# F. Frontend / UX Approach

## F.1 Framework recommendation

**Next.js 15 (App Router) + TypeScript + Tailwind + shadcn/ui, deployed as a PWA.**

Your current repo is a bare Vite + React 19 template with `motion` already installed — nothing to preserve, so this is a free choice.

**Why Next.js over staying on Vite/SPA:**
- Server components keep the initial bundle small, which matters on a rural 3G connection more than anywhere else.
- Route-level code splitting: the assistant never downloads the doctor portal or the admin console.
- Built-in image optimisation — you're handling wound photos and card scans, and unoptimised uploads on 3G will destroy the experience.
- API routes give you a clean BFF layer for anything that shouldn't hit the Express API directly.
- Vercel deployment is effectively zero-config, which matters when you're standing this up under time pressure.

**Trade-off:** more framework than a pure SPA needs, and the App Router's server/client boundary is a real learning curve — particularly around realtime subscriptions, which must live in client components. Manageable, and the payoff on load performance is worth it for this user base.

**Why one codebase and not native mobile:** §3.5 asks for responsive across mobile/tablet/desktop. A PWA covers all three, installs to a home screen, works offline, and needs no app-store process. For a government pilot, "the health worker opens a link and installs it" beats "wait for Play Store review." Revisit native only if you need Bluetooth on iOS, where Web Bluetooth is unsupported — worth flagging now: **iOS cannot do Web Bluetooth**, so the IoT demo must run on Android or desktop Chrome. Plan the demo device accordingly.

**Component and animation layer:**
- **shadcn/ui** — accessible Radix primitives you own the source of, so clinical components can be customised without fighting a library.
- **Framer Motion** (`motion`, already installed) — page transitions, tier-reveal choreography, queue animations.
- **React Three Fiber + drei** — the 3D on the landing page.
- **Recharts** or **visx** — vitals trends and the risk timeline.
- **TanStack Query** — server state, with Supabase Realtime pushing cache invalidations.

## F.2 The three surfaces

**Landing page (§3.9)** — this is the investor/government-facing artefact and deserves disproportionate polish.
- Hero with a subtle 3D scene: an abstract village-to-clinic-to-doctor connection, slow ambient motion, not a spinning logo. Restraint reads as expensive; spectacle reads as a student project.
- Scroll-driven narrative: the problem (distance, no doctor), the flow (capture → AI → triage → doctor), the impact.
- Background video behind a darkened overlay, muted, `prefers-reduced-motion` respected.
- Two clear entry points: **Doctor** and **Clinical Assistant**. Admin is not linked (§C.5).
- **Performance budget is a hard constraint:** the 3D scene must lazy-load below a low-power/slow-connection check, and there must be a static fallback. A landing page that stutters on the venue's projector laptop is worse than no 3D at all.

**Clinical Assistant portal** — this is where actual usability matters, and it should look different from the landing page: dense, fast, high-contrast, glove-and-sunlight friendly.
- **"Doctor feedback" section, pinned above everything else** (§0.6). Cases the doctor flagged back with a note. Visually distinct, badge-counted, and impossible to miss — this is a doctor correcting the care of a real patient, so it outranks every other item on the screen. Requires explicit acknowledgement (`assistant_acknowledged_at`) so a flag can't be scrolled past.
- **Prescriptions inbox** — doctor-issued PDFs from MEDIUM consultations (§E.2b), view + print.
- Recent-patients stack (5–10, recency-ordered) as the default view, per §3.9.
- Large touch targets, high contrast, minimal chrome. Assume a cheap Android tablet in a bright room.
- The capture flow as a clear progressive stepper: identity → vitals → symptoms → history → documents → assess. Every step skippable, because the emergency path (§3.8) must never be blocked by a required field.
- Voice input as a **primary, prominent** control, not an icon hidden in a corner. For this user, voice is the main input mode, and the UI should say so.
- Persistent offline indicator with a pending-sync count.

**Doctor portal** — information-dense, review-optimised.
- Day-wise LOW-case review queue with keyboard-driven **approve / flag-back-to-assistant / refer** (§0.6), because a doctor clearing 30 cases needs throughput, not delight. Approve should be a single keystroke; flagging opens a note field, since the note is mandatory.
- **Prescription issuer** — structured form → generated PDF, or upload (§E.2b). Reachable directly from a completed consultation.
- Consultation queue with countdown timers on the 5-minute tolerance window.
- **AI output and doctor decision visually separated** — not merely differently coloured, but structurally distinct panes with explicit attribution ("AI suggested" / "Your decision"). This is §2's requirement expressed in UI.
- Realtime updates that animate in rather than snapping, so it's obvious on stage that data is live across devices.

## F.3 Tier visual language

The tier is the most important piece of information on screen and its treatment should be unmistakable — while avoiding the trap of building an interface that reads as alarming when it shouldn't.

| Tier | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **LOW** | Calm green accent, standard layout. Sections: first aid → patient details → medications (marked *pending doctor review*) → point-wise precautions → optional diet. The "pending review" badge is essential — it's the visible form of "AI suggestion ≠ medical decision." |
| **MEDIUM** | Amber accent, elevated urgency. Consultation scheduling card is the primary element, with assigned doctor, countdown, and join control. First aid remains prominent because the assistant acts *now*, while waiting. |
| **HIGH** | **Danger-zone state.** Full-screen mode change — deep red field, larger type, reduced density, everything non-essential stripped out. Referral card (nearest matching hospital, distance, contact, bed availability), first aid, point-wise precautions, and a prominent **Generate & Print Bill** action. Persists until billing completes, then clears with a deliberate transition (§3.6). |

**Two cautions on the HIGH state:**
1. **Accessibility** — red-on-red fails contrast easily, and roughly 8% of men have some colour vision deficiency. Pair colour with iconography, a text label, and layout change. Never encode tier in colour alone.
2. **Don't make it frightening to the patient.** The assistant's screen may be visible to a scared person and their family. Urgent and clear, not cinematic. This is a judgement call worth making deliberately — the prototype's "reddish screen" instinct is right, but it should read as *clinical urgency*, not *alarm*.

## F.4 Offline — DEFERRED to the next version (per §0.2)

**Not built now.** Two structural things to do anyway, because they cost ~nothing today and are expensive to retrofit:

- **One client-side data-access module** for every write. The offline queue later slots in behind it, instead of being threaded through 40 components.
- **Optimistic UI on clinical writes** — advance the flow, reconcile on response. Good UX on a slow rural link regardless of offline support.

**Build now (one day, high value):** graceful degradation. A dropped connection mid-capture shows *"Reconnecting — your data is safe"* and retries, rather than losing the form. This is the most likely on-stage failure and the cheapest to prevent.

**Deferred to the next version:** service worker + app-shell caching, IndexedDB write queue, per-record sync status, and conflict resolution. When that lands, note now for the future implementer: last-write-wins is acceptable for demographics but **never for clinical data** — two assistants recording different vitals for one visit must surface for human resolution, not silently discard one.

## F.5 Accessibility and localisation

- WCAG 2.1 AA as the baseline. This is a government-facing product; accessibility compliance is likely to be a procurement requirement, not a nicety.
- All strings via `locales/` (i18next), including the tier explanations and first-aid text. Right-to-left support for Urdu.
- Numerals: Indian digit grouping (lakh/crore) where appropriate.
- Icon + text always, never icon alone — literacy varies.
- Large-text mode toggle, since assistants may be older.

## G.3 Migration path — why it isn't a rewrite

This is the design property §G asks for, and it holds because of choices made in §A:

- **Database:** Supabase Cloud → self-hosted Postgres is `pg_dump`/`pg_restore`. Schema, RLS policies and functions are all standard Postgres. No application change.
- **Auth:** Supabase Auth is GoTrue, self-hostable, and the JWT contract stays identical.
- **Storage:** S3-compatible on both sides; migration is a bucket sync plus an endpoint change.
- **API:** already containerised; move the container.
- **Frontend:** Next.js runs on Vercel or in any container.
- **Realtime:** the one genuine coupling. Mitigate by putting realtime access behind a thin adapter in `sockets/` from day one, so swapping Supabase Realtime for a self-hosted alternative touches one module.

The migration is a weekend of careful work, not a rewrite. That is precisely the answer to give an incubator or an enterprise buyer who asks about lock-in.
